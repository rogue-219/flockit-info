COMMUNITY PROFILE

Michigan City, Indiana

Michigan City's published Flock Safety agreements document purchases of license plate reader and Raven audio-detection services. This profile separates what the records establish from reporting, current operational questions, and evidence still needed.

WHAT DOES THE SYSTEM COLLECT?

Your vehicle has a “fingerprint.”

Michigan City's agreement says Flock can identify vehicles using more than a license plate number.

Vehicle type Make Color State registration Missing or covered plate Bumper stickers Decals Roof racks Bike racks

The agreement calls these characteristics a “Vehicle Fingerprint.” Authorized users can search recorded vehicle data using characteristics such as color, make, decals, bumper stickers, and vehicle accessories, not just a license plate number.

How long is the data kept?

The 2022 agreement (page 3) describes ordinary rolling 30-day deletion of Flock-hosted footage and agency extraction, downloading, and archiving. Section 4.2 (page 10) permits saving or transmitting footage before deletion and specifies seven days for Wing Replay; its inclusion in standard terms does not establish local deployment. Section 4.1 (page 9) also addresses preservation in specified legal circumstances. The 2024 order lists 30-day retention. This is not a universal deletion deadline for exported evidence, audit logs, or every data type. The agreement separately addresses aggregated data in §4.5; current settings, amendments, and agency retention practices remain unverified.

THE UNRESOLVED QUESTION

What permissions currently exist, in which direction, and what searches actually occurred? The agreement's §4.2 (page 10) contemplates law-enforcement hotlist monitoring and investigative search access. That contractual permission does not establish actual access by any particular agency. The July 2026 statement attributed to MCPD and the portal's current configuration need to be checked against dated sources.

RENEWAL & TERMINATION

Two orders, different renewal provisions.

Original 2022 order

The order (page 1) specifies a 24-month renewal term. Under §6.1 (page 11), Falcon and Raven terms commence upon first installation and validation of the relevant hardware. Non-renewal requires at least 30 days' notice before the applicable term ends.

2024 camera addition

The order (page 2) specifies a 24-month initial term and 12-month renewal terms, with at least 30 days' notice of non-renewal. It incorporates the previously executed agreement's terms. Its execution date is not, by itself, evidence of installation and validation.

Actual renewal dates and non-renewal deadlines are not established. The published collection does not establish the relevant commencement dates, subsequent amendments, or term adjustments. Approval dates, signatures, grant dates, and annual invoices should not be substituted for that evidence. Section 2.7.2 of the original agreement also addresses term tolling for specified service interruptions. Section 9.11 (pages 15–16) governs notice delivery.

Unresolved invoice term discrepancy

Flock invoice INV-80747, dated December 4, 2025, describes Michigan City's service as “Co-Term: Year 3 of 60 Month Term, 2025–2026.” The signed 2022 order lists a 24-month initial term and 24-month renewal periods, while the 2024 addition lists a 24-month initial term and 12-month renewal periods. The invoice note does not, by itself, establish an amended 60-month contract.

FlockIt.info requested clarification from the City's Deputy City Attorney on October 21, 2026, concerning any additional agreement, the original installation and validation dates, and the current expiration and notice deadlines. The request was resent from another email address on October 23 following a reported email-filtering issue. These questions remain unresolved pending a documented response.

Non-renewal is not the only provision to examine.

The original §6.2 (page 11) permits agency termination for convenience effective immediately, with a $500 removal fee per Flock Hardware and stated prorated refund provisions. Wing Suite products are excluded from early-termination refunds. Physical removal occurs later within a commercially reasonable period.

The 2024 order (page 3) has separate language limiting termination after its 60-day opt-out period, except as provided in the agreement. These provisions and any amendments must be reconciled before claiming identical cancellation rights for both orders or quoting a total removal cost.

Original §6.4 says certain complimentary hotlist and non-agency access can survive the paid term and has separate termination provisions. Ending a paid subscription should not be presented as proof that every form of Flock access has ended.

KNOW THE TECHNOLOGY

“Flock cameras” are not all the same thing.

The published orders and historical expansion draft describe several different surveillance technologies; the draft is not proof those additional products were deployed. They collect different kinds of information and create different capabilities.

LICENSE PLATE READERS

ALPR / Flock Falcon

Automated license plate readers capture vehicles as they pass. Flock's LPR system records plate information and vehicle characteristics such as make, color, body type, date, time, and camera location. The resulting records can be searched later and can generate alerts when a vehicle matches a hot list.

How Flock describes its LPR technology

VIDEO SURVEILLANCE

PTZ cameras

PTZ means Pan-Tilt-Zoom. These are live and recorded video cameras that an operator can remotely point and zoom. Flock describes them for monitoring areas such as intersections, parking lots, parks, trails, and other public spaces. They are video surveillance cameras, not simply another type of license plate reader.

How Flock describes its video cameras

AUDIO DETECTION

Raven / Gunshot Detection

Flock's gunshot and audio detection technology analyzes sound for events such as gunfire and can generate an alert when a qualifying sound is detected. It is a different sensor and data system from both license plate readers and video cameras.

How Flock describes its audio detection technology

AERIAL VIDEO

Drone as First Responder

Drone as First Responder systems allow remotely operated drones to be deployed to calls for service or other alerts and provide live aerial video to police and dispatch. This creates a different surveillance capability from a fixed roadside camera.

How Flock describes Drone as First Responder

WHY THE DISTINCTION MATTERS

When these technologies are discussed together as “Flock cameras,” important differences can disappear. A license plate reader, a remotely controlled video camera, an acoustic sensor, and a drone do different things. Understanding those capabilities is necessary to evaluate what safeguards should apply to each one.

WHO MAKES THE RULES?

Not every safeguard has the same permanence.

When officials say safeguards are in place, it matters where those safeguards come from and who has the authority to change them.

REPORTED AUDIT · SOURCE VERIFICATION NEEDED

Earlier site reporting described a September 4, 2026 MCPD announcement of an audit, an internal investigation, an officer's administrative leave and resignation, and a departmental policy-violation finding. A direct release or official copy is not linked in the published collection, so those details and any later disposition remain independently unverified in this review.

A verified release, current use policy, and audit procedures are needed to establish the department's findings and continuing safeguards. An internal policy finding should not be equated with a criminal conviction.

THE VENDOR

Flock's platform controls

Flock builds technical controls into its products, including user accounts, search fields, permissions, and audit capabilities. These controls are part of the vendor's platform and can change as the product changes.

THE POLICE DEPARTMENT

MCPD policy

Police Department policy can establish how officers are expected to use the technology, including what kinds of searches are permitted and what information must be entered when a search is conducted. Department policies can generally be revised by the department.

THE CONTRACT

What Michigan City requires from Flock

Contract terms can establish obligations between the City and the vendor, including pricing, retention, access, renewal, and other conditions. Those protections are tied to the particular agreement and vendor.

CITY GOVERNMENT

Public rules established in law

The Common Council can establish broader rules through ordinance and City Code. Those rules can govern surveillance technology regardless of which department operates it or which company supplies it.

THE GOVERNANCE QUESTION

Which safeguards should depend on a vendor or internal department policy, and which should be established publicly by elected government so they remain in place even if the technology, vendor, or leadership changes?

ONE GOVERNANCE MODEL

Community Control Over Police Surveillance

Some communities have adopted laws commonly known as CCOPS, or Community Control Over Police Surveillance. The basic idea is that decisions about surveillance technology should not rest only with a police department or technology vendor.

BEFORE ADOPTION

Public review and approval

The CCOPS model calls for information about proposed surveillance technology, its intended uses, costs, and policies to be made public before elected officials decide whether to approve it.

AFTER ADOPTION

Ongoing public oversight

The model also calls for written use policies and continuing public reporting so elected officials and residents can evaluate how approved surveillance technology is actually being used.

A MODEL, NOT THE ONLY ANSWER

FlockIt.info presents CCOPS as one example of how communities have approached surveillance governance. Michigan City could adopt this model, create its own framework, or choose another approach. The underlying question is who should establish the rules and how much public oversight should exist.
