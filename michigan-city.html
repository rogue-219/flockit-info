COMMUNITY PROFILE

Michigan City, Indiana

Michigan City uses Flock Safety technology as part of its law enforcement operations. This page tracks what the public record tells us about the city's agreements, costs, capabilities, data practices, and oversight — and what questions still need answers.

Michigan City has used Flock Safety technology since 2022. The city's system includes automated license plate readers and Raven gunshot detection technology. The existing agreement automatically renews unless the city takes action to end it.

What does it cost?

Michigan City's existing Flock agreement represents an ongoing public expense, separate from the additional $300,000 expansion considered by the Common Council in 2026. We're documenting the original contract, subsequent costs, and proposed expansion separately so residents can see exactly what has been spent and what additional spending has been proposed.

The agreement

Michigan City's agreement with Flock Safety includes an automatic renewal provision. That means continuing the relationship does not necessarily require a new public decision each year. Unless the city acts within the contract's required notice period, the agreement can continue into another term.

WHAT DOES THE SYSTEM COLLECT?

Your vehicle has a “fingerprint.”

Michigan City's agreement says Flock can identify vehicles using more than a license plate number.

Vehicle type Make Color State registration Missing or covered plate Bumper stickers Decals Roof racks Bike racks

The agreement calls these characteristics a “Vehicle Fingerprint.” Authorized users can search recorded vehicle data using characteristics such as color, make, decals, bumper stickers, and vehicle accessories, not just a license plate number.

How long is the data kept?

The agreement states that Flock deletes captured footage on a rolling 30-day basis unless the parties agree otherwise in writing. That means the system can maintain a searchable record of ordinary vehicle activity for up to 30 days, not just information associated with vehicles suspected of a crime.

DATA SHARING & ACCESS

MCPD says the data stays local.

WHAT MCPD SAID

ABC57 reported that Assistant Chief Kyle Shiparski told the Common Council on July 21, 2026, that camera data is accessed when an investigation is triggered, deleted after 30 days, and “would not be shared outside of the Michigan City Police Department.”

WHAT FLOCK'S PORTAL SHOWS

Michigan City's Flock transparency portal lists hundreds of outside organizations as having access to Michigan City Police Department data, including local, state, and federal agencies.

THE UNRESOLVED QUESTION

How do those outside-agency access permissions reconcile with MCPD's public statement that the data would not be shared outside the department?

When does the agreement renew?

The exact renewal date is currently unknown. The agreement ties the start of the camera term to when the equipment was installed and validated, but the records produced by the city did not include a document establishing that date. The agreement automatically renews unless either party gives notice of non-renewal at least 30 days before the end of the then-current term.

Proposed expansion

In 2026, the city proposed using $300,000 from its Rainy Day Fund for a revised expansion of public safety technology. The proposal includes Flock's Drone as First Responder technology, strategically placed Pan-Tilt-Zoom cameras in the Franklin Street corridor, Community Partnership Cameras, and a Flock Safety Trailer equipped with mobile license plate reader and PTZ capabilities. The revised plan eliminated 20 fixed Live View cameras that had been included in an earlier proposal.

As justification for the additional spending, the proposed ordinance states that the existing Flock license plate reader and Raven gunshot detection systems are “frequently used” by MCPD and have become “integral to patrol operations and criminal investigations.” The ordinance does not provide supporting performance data for that claim.

What we still don't know

Important questions remain unanswered in the public record we've reviewed. We have not yet established the exact date the existing agreement will next renew, how often Flock information materially advances an investigation, or how often it is essential to an outcome rather than one of several investigative tools. We're also continuing to document Michigan City's current data-sharing relationships and oversight practices.
