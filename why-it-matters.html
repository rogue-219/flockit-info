WHY IT MATTERS

A license plate is just the beginning.

Flock systems can collect more than a plate number. They can record vehicle characteristics, location, time, and other searchable details. Once those records are retained, searched, and shared, the issue becomes larger than what one camera saw.

01 — WHAT GETS COLLECTED

Your vehicle can become searchable by its characteristics.

Flock contracts can define a “Vehicle Fingerprint” using characteristics such as vehicle type, make, color, state registration, bumper stickers, decals, roof racks, bike racks, and whether a plate is missing or covered.

That means a search does not have to begin with a known plate number. A vehicle can potentially be found by combinations of visible characteristics.

The important question is not whether the technology can be useful. It is whether residents understand how much ordinary vehicle information is being collected and made searchable.

02 — RETENTION

Real-time identification and historical tracking are different things.

A camera can alert police when a wanted vehicle passes by. That is a real-time use.

Retaining ordinary vehicle observations creates something different: a historical record that can be searched later.

That raises a basic policy question. If the central justification is identifying vehicles connected to crimes, how much historical information about everyone else's vehicles needs to be retained, and for how long?

03 — THE NETWORK EFFECT

The camera may be local. The search may not be.

Flock says there is no single national database. That distinction is important, but it does not answer the entire practical question.

Local systems can be connected through agency sharing and cross-jurisdiction search permissions. A network of interconnected local databases can therefore provide access far beyond the city that purchased the camera.

The better question is not simply whether a national database exists. It is how far a vehicle search can reach in practice, which agencies can perform it, and what controls govern that access.

WHAT THIS CAN LOOK LIKE IN PRACTICE

Michigan City illustrates why the details matter.

In July 2026, ABC57 reported that Michigan City Assistant Police Chief Kyle Shiparski said camera data “would not be shared outside of the Michigan City Police Department.”

Flock Safety's own transparency portal for Michigan City, however, lists hundreds of outside organizations as having access to Michigan City Police Department data, including local, state, and federal agencies.

That does not by itself establish that every listed agency searched Michigan City's data. It does raise a basic oversight question: what does “not shared outside the department” mean when outside-agency access permissions exist?

04 — EFFECTIVENESS

“Helped solve a crime” is not the same as “the crime would not have been solved without it.”

Police departments often point to cases where Flock information was useful. That matters.

But there is an important difference between a system being used, a system contributing useful information, and a system being essential to an outcome.

If a community is being asked to spend substantial public money, expand surveillance, or accept additional data collection, residents should be able to ask how often the technology materially advances an investigation and how often it is truly indispensable.

05 — PUBLIC OVERSIGHT

Useful to police is not automatically the same as necessary enough to justify the tradeoffs.

A technology can be useful and still deserve limits, review, and public debate.

Communities should know who approved the system, what it costs, when the contract renews, how long records are retained, who can access them, what searches are permitted, and whether those searches are audited.

Automatic renewal makes that oversight especially important. If a contract continues unless the government acts, renewal can become the default before the public ever has another meaningful discussion about whether the agreement should continue.

FlockIt.info exists to make those decisions easier to examine using contracts, ordinances, policies, public records, meeting materials, and other source documents.
